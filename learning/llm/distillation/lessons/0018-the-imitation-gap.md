# Lesson 18. The Imitation Gap

**Mission link:** The mission asks you to predict what a student will and will not pick up, and to review a "matches the teacher" claim, which means knowing how a student can look like its teacher while lacking what the teacher knows.
**Primary source:** [Paper: "The False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs", Gudibande et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15717), sections 1 and 4
**Prerequisites:** [Lesson 10](0010-distilling-from-generated-data.md), [Lesson 17](0017-fidelity-and-generalization.md), [Imitation gap](../GLOSSARY.md), [Synthetic data](../GLOSSARY.md), [Fidelity](../GLOSSARY.md)

2. ▢ Lesson 17 says task accuracy cannot stand in for agreement with the teacher. What is the reverse risk: can a student agree with its teacher on outputs and still lack its capability?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

Agreement is measured on the inputs you test, so it only speaks for those inputs. A student can match the teacher's phrasing on common requests and fail on tasks the training data hardly covered, which is the case this lesson examines.

</details>

3. ▢ Lesson 12 says volume does not fix bad examples. Name one thing that more teacher-generated data cannot supply to a student.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

Knowledge the student's own pretraining did not give it. More examples can teach format and behaviour, but they cannot cheaply make up for what the base model never learned. That claim is the core of this lesson.

</details>

## Know this

### The setup

Gudibande et al. study "model imitation": collect a dataset of outputs from a proprietary model through its API and fine-tune an open model on it. They imitate ChatGPT with models from 1.5B to 13B parameters (GPT-2 1.5B, LLaMA 7B and 13B) and imitation data from 0.3M to 150M tokens. The student sees only text, so this is sequence-level distillation without logits.

Two kinds of imitation matter to the argument:

| Kind | What it imitates | Their data |
|---|---|---|
| Local imitation | One task or domain | NQ-synthetic: 6,000 question-answer pairs generated by ChatGPT from a seed of 10 pairs, in the style of Natural Questions |
| Broad imitation | The whole range of behaviour | ShareGPT-Mix: about 50K deduplicated dialogues from ShareGPT, plus about 27K ChatGPT responses from HC3 and 10K examples from Discord ChatGPT bots |

### What the human raters saw

Crowd workers ran blind pairwise comparisons of each imitation model against ChatGPT, using about 70 workers on 255 held-out prompts. About 70% of the imitation models' outputs were rated as equal to or better than ChatGPT's (Figure 1 caption). GPT-4 as a judge showed the same trends. On those ratings, the imitation models looked close to the model they copied.

The ratings also saturated: more imitation data stopped raising them (section 4.2).

### What the benchmarks saw

The authors then ran targeted automatic evaluations: MMLU (5-shot), Natural Questions (3-shot) and HumanEval (0-shot). They report that across every benchmark measured, the broad-imitation models did not improve over their base models, or declined, even as imitation data grew (section 4.3, Figure 4).

The task-specific case is where it worked. Natural Questions accuracy from Table 1:

| Model | No imitation | Trained on ShareGPT-Mix (broad) | Trained on NQ-synthetic (local) | ChatGPT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LLaMA 7B | 17 | 10 | 22 | 31 |
| LLaMA 13B | 20 | 15 | 27 | 31 |

Broad imitation lowered accuracy and local imitation raised it, closing a large share of the gap on the one task it targeted. The authors' conclusion is that distilling one specific behaviour is far more feasible than matching ChatGPT's capabilities broadly. They also found that training on more ShareGPT-Mix data hurt some evaluations relative to the base model, which they attribute to a distribution shift between conversational fine-tuning data and benchmarks, and they flag as unresolved whether regularisation or mixing in pretraining data would fix it.

### Why the ratings and the benchmarks disagree

The paper's answer is that imitation models learn style, not content. Table 2 compares outputs of imitation models trained on 20M, 80M and 150M tokens with the base LLaMA and with a second ChatGPT sample:

| Metric | Base LLaMA | 20M | 150M | ChatGPT second sample |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| If ChatGPT outputs a list, does the model? | 13% | 50% | 81% | 83% |
| Outputs in an authoritative tone, per GPT-4 | 57% | 99% | 98% | 98% |
| Pearson correlation of length with ChatGPT's output | -0.11 | 0.51 | 0.62 | 0.67 |

The paper's table also has an 80M column, omitted here. On these style measures, the imitation models approach what a second ChatGPT answer scores. On factuality they remain weak. The paper's Figure 2 shows an imitation answer that has ChatGPT's structure and is completely wrong, in a confident tone.

The authors' account of why raters missed it: crowd workers without domain expertise or significant time can be deceived by confident, well-structured answers. They draw two lessons for evaluation. Use domain experts or hard prompts, and note that GPT-4 as judge showed the same bias.

### What scaling does and does not buy

This is the claim to get exactly right. The authors say the gap can, with current methods, be bridged only by an unwieldy amount of imitation data or by a more capable base model. Their evidence for the second half: increasing base model size improved the automatic benchmarks steadily, while increasing imitation data gave little improvement or regressions (Figure 4). Their conclusion is that the higher-leverage action is a better base model.

Two qualifications matter. The "unwieldy amount of imitation data" part is an argument: they say matching ChatGPT broadly would need a dataset closer to the scale of pretraining, and note it is unclear whether that is worth doing. They did not run it. And base model size is a proxy for base model quality, a point their own footnote makes. The setting is also 2023 LLaMA and GPT-2 base models imitating ChatGPT, so do not treat the sizes as current.

### What does transfer

The paper also reports what a student does pick up. Imitation models became less toxic on RealToxicityPrompts as imitation data grew, which the authors read as the model learning to abstain in the way ChatGPT does (Figure 5). They conclude imitation is effective when you have a strong base model and want to avoid annotating fine-tuning data. A student inherits behaviours the teacher was trained to show, including refusal patterns, much more cheaply than it inherits knowledge.

### Caveats the authors state

They name three: ChatGPT's evaluation scores may be inflated by contamination in an unknown pretraining set, they used only supervised imitation and not RLHF or constitutional methods, and they used simple data collection. One more caution is mine: the evaluation used 255 prompts after filtering out coding and non-English ones, so the rating result is narrower than "ChatGPT quality".

## Practice

1. ▢ Using Table 1, how much of the gap to ChatGPT on Natural Questions does local imitation close for LLaMA 13B and for LLaMA 7B, and what did broad imitation do?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

For 13B the gap is 31 minus 20, which is 11, and NQ-synthetic adds 7 points, about 64% of it. For 7B the gap is 14 and it adds 5, about 36%. Broad ShareGPT-Mix imitation lowered accuracy in both, by 7 points for 7B and 5 for 13B. These are NQ numbers on one benchmark, not a statement about all tasks.

</details>

2. ▢ Why did crowd workers rate the imitation models close to ChatGPT while benchmarks showed little gain?

    - a) Raters knew each model's identity
    - b) Raters verified every claim independently
    - c) Raters favoured sheer answer length
    - d) Raters rewarded confident polished style

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

**d)** The paper says imitation models copy ChatGPT's style, and raters without domain expertise or time to check facts can be deceived by it. (b) is the opposite of what happened. (c) is not the paper's explanation, though the models' output length did correlate with ChatGPT's. (a) is wrong: the comparison was blind.

</details>

3. ▢ A team fine-tunes a 7B model on 200K more ChatGPT-style dialogues and reports a gain from crowd ratings. What two things do you ask for before believing the model matches the teacher?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Think about which kind of measurement the paper found diverged from the ratings, and what was missing from the tasks.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

Ask for targeted automatic benchmarks on tasks the training data does not heavily cover, such as factual knowledge, coding or reasoning, against the base model before fine-tuning. Ask who the raters were and whether any had domain expertise or time to check facts. The paper's finding is that ratings can rise while benchmarks stay flat or fall. Ratings alone prove only that the style transferred.

</details>

4. ▢ You can spend a fixed budget on either a million more broad teacher-written examples or moving to a base model twice the size. What does the paper's evidence suggest, and what does it not show?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

In its experiments, scaling the base model improved benchmarks steadily while scaling imitation data gave little or negative change, which suggests the base model. It does not show that no amount of broad imitation data would help, since they did not test data close to pretraining scale, and it does not cover a task-specific case, where their local imitation worked well. For one narrow task the data may be the better spend.

</details>

5. ▢ Lesson 13 asked what a provider's terms allow. The paper lists reasons for imitation from benign to illegal, including companies launching services that compete with the proprietary system. Why does that matter for a ShareGPT-style dataset?

<details markdown="1"><summary>Check</summary>

Because a dataset of another provider's outputs is only usable if its terms permit it. For example, OpenAI's Terms of Use list, among things a user cannot do, using output to develop models that compete with OpenAI (rest-of-world terms, effective January 1, 2026). Check the current text for the provider you used. I did not find an analysis of whether any particular dataset was permitted in the sections of the paper I read.

</details>
