# SQL Notes

Working notes for the teaching session. Not linked from `README.md`.

## Preferences

- **Lessons are written for a general reader, deliberately.** No machine, OS, client tool or installed version appears in any lesson, reference sheet, `README.md` or `RESOURCES.md`. Requested explicitly; do not personalise the lesson material to whoever is running the session.
- Mission is the full arc: zero to senior specialist, assuming no prior SQL. Start at stage 1 rather than calibrating against a particular reader.
- Reps must fit one sitting, with one exception noted below: stage 6 needs a dataset that takes longer to prepare than to query.

## State

**The arc is complete: 48 lessons and nine reference sheets, stages 1 to 7.** The sheets are `null-and-three-valued-logic.md`, `select-evaluation-order.md`, `the-dataset.md`, `querying.md`, `beyond-the-basics.md`, `schema-design.md`, `transactions.md`, `performance.md` and `operating.md`.

Stage 1's six lessons use one schema, `customers` and `orders`, introduced in lesson 0001. Stage 2 added `countries` beside them and nothing else, and stage 3 added a separate four-row `events` table for the JSON lesson alone; see "The dataset, settled". Keep using it. Its nullability is deliberate: `country` is unknown-if-absent and `shipped_at` is not-yet-if-absent, which gives lesson 0003 two genuinely different meanings of `NULL` to work with, and gives later stages a foreign key and a money column to reason about.

**How the glossary is populated here.** The skill's test, that a term lands once it can be used correctly, is about a learner's demonstration. These lessons have no single learner, so the test is applied to the material: a term lands when a lesson has taught it well enough for a reader to use it. Stage 1 added seven terms alongside the three pinned ones. Keep doing this per stage, and do not add a term the lessons have not earned.

## Engine policy

**Baseline, rechecked before stages 2 to 7, unchanged every time: PostgreSQL 18.6, the current minor of the current major.** The supported majors are 14 to 18 and 13 went out of support, so a claim checked on 18 is a claim about every supported release only if it does not depend on a recent feature. Stages 2 to 7 were written and verified on 18.6. SQLite claims were checked on 3.51, and stage 5 deliberately made no SQLite claim it had not run. Recheck both before the next stage rather than assuming these still hold, and name the version in a lesson only where the claim depends on it.

PostgreSQL is the reference engine and SQLite is the no-server alternative. Both choices are about documentation quality rather than popularity: PostgreSQL documents its planner and its concurrency model in public, and SQLite's planner is simple enough to see through.

The risk is teaching PostgreSQL and calling it SQL. Two defences, and they need applying per lesson rather than once:

- Check Modern SQL before claiming something is standard. The instinct to say "the standard requires" is usually wrong.
- Where behaviour is engine-specific, say which engine in the lesson body, not in a footnote.

## The dataset, settled

**Resolved before stage 2, and published as `reference/the-dataset.md`.** The decision was forced earlier than the original note expected: stage 2 cannot teach joins without a third table to join to, so the dataset had to exist here rather than during stage 4.

What was decided, and why each part was decided that way.

- **Generated, never downloaded**, by SQL the reader can read. A downloaded dataset dates, moves and eventually 404s, and it puts a claim in a lesson that nobody can check.
- **Deterministic, with no `random()` and no seed.** Every generated value is arithmetic on the row's own number. Verified rather than assumed: loading both scripts into an empty database twice produced byte-identical tables, checked as an `md5` over the concatenation of every row of `customers` and of `orders`. Had `setseed` been used instead, determinism would have depended on the planner's parallelism, which is not a promise worth teaching against.
- **Two sizes, one schema.** A small fixture of eight customers and twelve orders, checkable by eye, which every lesson from stage 2 quotes; and a large fixture of 100,000 customers and 1,049,916 orders, for stage 6. The large one is the small one plus generated rows, so an identifier named in a lesson means the same row in both.
- **One new table, `countries`.** Stage 1's `customers` and `orders` are untouched. The lookup table is what makes a join teachable: it holds five countries with no customers, and customer 5's code `NL` is absent from it, so an outer join has something to report and an inner join loses a customer who exists.
- **Every awkward row is deliberate** and listed in the sheet's table: a customer with no orders, two `NULL` countries, four unshipped orders, and two orders with the same amount. A dataset where everything matches teaches nothing about the cases that produce wrong answers.

The size question was settled by measurement rather than by picking a round number. On the large fixture with statistics collected, one customer's orders go from a parallel sequential scan discarding about 350,000 rows per worker to an index-only scan reading 7 rows with no heap fetches, while a predicate matching a third of the table keeps its two sequential scans and its hash join even with that index present. Both halves are the evidence: the first says the dataset is big enough to teach with, the second says it is big enough to teach honestly. Generation takes a few seconds, so it stays inside the one-sitting rule.

Skew is on purpose: about a third of customers are `US`, a sixth `GB`, an eighth each in six others, one percent have no country, and exactly one is `NL`. One column therefore holds a value too common for an index to help and a value rare enough for it to decide the plan.

## Stage 4's decision: where a schema lesson does its damage

Stage 4 had no blocking decision recorded against it, and it turned out to have one anyway, which is where the redesigning happens. Settled before writing, on evidence.

**The published fixture is not touched, and stage 4 adds nothing to it.** Every row count in lessons 0007 to 0020 is a claim about `customers`, `orders` and `countries` exactly as `reference/the-dataset.md` prints them, and the dataset sheet promises that schema is what stages 4 to 7 reason about. A stage that normalises or re-keys those tables would invalidate published arithmetic in thirteen lessons, so it does not.

**The stage teaches on its own throwaway schema instead, created and dropped inside the lesson.** That is not a workaround, it is what the material needs: the fixture is already well designed, since lesson 0001 chose the types deliberately and lesson 0006 added the keys and constraints, so it has none of the defects normalisation removes. A schema lesson needs a schema with problems in it. Verified before committing to the approach, on 18.6: a deliberately denormalised signups table reproduces all three classic anomalies for real, not as a story. One `UPDATE` that set a new country name for `GB` touched three rows and left the table with two spellings, because the fourth row held a typo the predicate never matched. One plan appeared under two spellings differing only in case, carrying two different prices. One email appeared twice with no constraint to stop it. A transitive dependency reproduced the same way: updating one employee's department head left one department with two heads.

**Where normalisation stops, and why that boundary rather than a tidier one.** The stage teaches first, second and third normal form and names Boyce-Codd once with a demonstration, and stops there. The reason is the arc's own rule that a claim carries the evidence that produced it: each of those violations can be shown as a query whose result is wrong, and each fix can be shown to make the wrong result impossible. Fourth and fifth normal form are named as existing, with a pointer, and not taught, because this stage cannot produce a violation of either on a schema a reader would recognise. That is a limit of the teaching method rather than a claim that they do not matter.

## Stage 5's decisions: how a concurrency anomaly gets shown

Two decisions, both settled before writing and both on evidence.

**An anomaly is sequenced, not raced, and every one of them is deterministic.** The recorded instruction for this stage said to reproduce every anomaly with attempt counts, the way the Java arc handled data races. That instruction was wrong for this subject, and the evidence is that a two-session harness which interleaves statements in a fixed order reproduces **every** anomaly on the first attempt, every time: a non-repeatable read, a phantom, a lost update, write skew, a serialization failure, a deadlock. A database anomaly is not a race in the Java sense, because the interleaving that produces it is one a client can dictate statement by statement. So the honest report here is "one attempt, deterministic, in this order", and a lesson that said "reproduced three times in a hundred runs" would be describing a worse experiment rather than a more careful one. Where an outcome genuinely depends on timing, which happens only for which session a deadlock detector picks as its victim, the lesson says so.

**The reader reproduces it with two client sessions, and the lessons print a step table.** Every demonstration is written as an ordered list of steps labelled by session, so a reader with two terminals open can follow it exactly and see the same values. That format is the stage's convention: session name, statement, and what came back, in order, with a blocked step shown as blocked rather than silently skipped. It is what the harness does, so the lesson and the verification agree by construction.

**Stage 5 writes, so it works on its own table.** Concurrency material has to update rows, and the published fixture is what fourteen lessons quote counts from, so the stage creates its own two-row `accounts` table in the lesson body, the same convention stage 4 settled. Nothing in the fixture is written.

## Stage 6's decisions: what a performance lesson is allowed to quote

The stage had no recorded blocking decision and has an obvious one: a measured number is a fact about the machine that measured it, and these lessons are written for a reader with different hardware. Settled on evidence before writing.

**Plan-derived counts are the evidence; a timing is never a number to match.** Five runs of one identical query gave execution times from 16.021 to 17.213 milliseconds, a seven percent spread, while the buffer count was **7608 on every run**. So the numbers a lesson quotes are the ones the plan derives and repeats: rows, loops, buffers hit and read, `Heap Fetches`, `Index Searches`, hash `Batches` and `Memory Usage`, `Sort Method` and its disk figure. A timing may appear only as a ratio, with the workload's shape stated, and never as a value a reader should expect to reproduce. This is the arc's existing rule about measured numbers, made specific enough to enforce.

**The measurements run on the large fixture, with the four constraint-backed indexes and nothing else.** `countries_pkey`, `customers_pkey`, `customers_email_key` and `orders_pkey` are what the schema declares, and every secondary index in the stage is created by a lesson and dropped by it. That clean slate is the point: a stage about choosing an index cannot start with the answers already installed. Stage 2 had already measured that the plan flips at this size, which is why the fixture is 100,000 customers and 1,049,916 orders rather than something rounder.

**A claim that depends on a setting names the setting.** The server runs stock defaults, verified: `shared_buffers` 128MB, `work_mem` 4MB, `max_parallel_workers_per_gather` 2, `random_page_cost` 4, `seq_page_cost` 1, `track_io_timing` off. A reader shares those unless they have changed them, so the lessons state the value wherever it decides the plan, which is `work_mem` for whether a hash spills, `random_page_cost` for whether an index is worth reading, and the worker count for whether a `Gather` appears at all.

**PostgreSQL 18 changed `EXPLAIN`'s output in four ways, so every pre-18 tutorial looks different from what a reader sees.** Confirmed in that release's notes: `BUFFERS` is now included in `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` automatically, an index scan node reports the number of index lookups it performed, row counts are printed fractionally, and memory and disk details were added to more node types. The stage teaches the 18 shape and names the release, and the second of those changes turns out to be how the new skip-scan optimisation becomes visible at all.

## Stage 7's decisions: the missing source and the chosen ORM

Both were recorded as open when the arc was planned, and both are settled here on evidence.

**The source gap is real, and it splits in two rather than closing.** There is no canonical source for migrating a schema without downtime, and the search for one produced a useful shape instead of a single link. The **authoritative half is PostgreSQL's own reference**: `ALTER TABLE` states the lock each subclause takes, `CREATE INDEX` documents `CONCURRENTLY` and its restrictions, and the release notes date the two optimisations the whole subject turns on, verified by fetching them. Release 11's notes list "the ability to avoid a table rewrite for `ALTER TABLE ... ADD COLUMN` with a non-null column default", and release 12's list "Allow `ALTER TABLE ... SET NOT NULL` to avoid unnecessary table scans", adding that this happens "when the table's column constraints can be recognized as disallowing nulls". The **practitioner half, the catalogue of which migration is unsafe and what to write instead, exists only in maintained community lists**, and the most complete current one is the `strong_migrations` README, checked live: it enumerates removing a column, changing a type, renaming, adding a foreign key, adding a check constraint, backfilling, adding an index non-concurrently, adding a unique constraint, setting `NOT NULL` and renaming an enum value, with a PostgreSQL-specific section for each. Its caveat is stated in the lesson: it is a Ruby library's documentation, so the catalogue travels and the code does not. `pgroll` and the postgres.ai how-to series corroborate it. One thing worth recording as a negative result: the most-cited historical write-up on this, Braintree's, answers a scripted request with a 403, so it cannot be a source this arc links, and that is part of why the gap exists at all.

**Because the source is split, the stage verifies the mechanics rather than citing the list.** A lock level is observable, so the lessons observe it, and `pg_relation_filenode` is a hardware-independent way to see whether an `ALTER TABLE` rewrote the table, which is what "unsafe" usually means. Verified: adding a nullable column and adding a `NOT NULL` column with a constant default both leave the filenode unchanged, while a volatile default such as `clock_timestamp()` changes it, and so do `int` to `bigint`, `text` to `varchar(60)` and `bigint` to `numeric`, while `varchar(60)` to `varchar(80)` and `varchar` to `text` do not. The one claim the stage takes from the release notes rather than from a run is that a validated `CHECK` lets `SET NOT NULL` skip its scan, because proving a skipped scan needs a timing and this arc's stage 6 rule forbids quoting one.

**The ORM is SQLAlchemy, over the driver this workspace already uses, and it was chosen because it could be verified here.** The requirement was one concrete example from a real ORM, chosen once and reused, without teaching ORMs. SQLAlchemy 2.0.52 connects to the fixture through pg8000 and logs every statement it emits, so the lesson quotes SQL a real ORM really produced rather than a plausible reconstruction. Verified against the large fixture: fetching five customers and then touching each one's orders emits **six statements**, one for the customers and one per customer, which is the N+1 as it actually appears; `selectinload` answers the same question in **two**, the second carrying an `IN` list of the five ids; and `joinedload` answers it in **one**, as a `LEFT OUTER JOIN` against a subquery holding the `LIMIT`, which is why the application then has to deduplicate the rows. The application-side count is equally concrete: loading the rows to take their length emits `SELECT orders.id, orders.customer_id, orders.amount FROM orders WHERE orders.customer_id = ...` and counts in the client, where the same answer is one `SELECT count(*)`. Nothing about configuring SQLAlchemy is taught, and the lesson says which parts are that library's names for a pattern every ORM has.

## What execution changed

Stage 2's findings, all run on PostgreSQL 18.6 against the small fixture, with SQLite checks on 3.51. The harness was a local server the session started for the purpose; the scripts that build the data are published in `reference/the-dataset.md`, so a later stage can rebuild the same conditions from the workspace rather than from this file.

- **My own stage spec was wrong twice, and both were caught by an author running the query.** It told lesson 0009 that `avg(amount)` divides by 8 rather than 12 because of the `NULL`s; `amount` is `NOT NULL` and it divides by 12, so the lesson demonstrates the skipping with a three-row `VALUES` example instead and keeps the true `orders` numbers. And it labelled a set of counts as coming from `customers FULL JOIN countries` when they came from `orders FULL JOIN customers`, so `count(shipped_at)` named a column the labelled join does not have. Neither reached a published file. The lesson for the next stage: a spec fact needs the query beside it, not just the number.
- **The fan-out bug is worth the space it gets.** Joining `customers` to `orders` and then to `countries` on `region = 'Europe'` gives 36 rows and a `sum(amount)` of 7219.47 against a true 2406.49, exactly three times too big. The author also verified that the obvious repair does not work: `sum(DISTINCT amount)` gives 2396.49, wrong in the other direction, because two orders genuinely cost `10.00` and deduplication throws one away. That pair of numbers is the argument.
- **`GROUPING SETS` and `ROLLUP` produce a total row whose grouping column is `NULL`**, printing identically to the real group of customers with no country, so the output holds two blank rows meaning different things and `grouping()` is the only way to separate them. Verified, and it is why the grouping material sits in a lesson rather than in the sheet.
- **The CTE folklore is out of date and the stage says so.** On 18.6 a `WITH` referenced once is inlined and its plan is identical to the same query written as a derived table, with no `CTE` node; `MATERIALIZED` puts the fence back; and a CTE referenced twice is materialised without being asked. Lesson 0012 dates the change to PostgreSQL 12 from that release's notes, which list automatic but overridable inlining of common table expressions.
- **A subquery in `FROM` no longer needs an alias**, since PostgreSQL 16, verified by running it without one. The lessons teach writing the alias and name the version, because a reader on 14 or 15 gets a syntax error and would otherwise read it as their own mistake.
- **An error message echoes the token in the case the author wrote it.** Lesson 0013 quoted `syntax error at or near "union"` under an example written in upper case; the same query gives `"UNION"`. Found by the sheet writer, verified centrally, corrected. Quote a diagnostic from the run of the exact text the lesson prints, not from a variant.
- **`NATURAL JOIN` on this schema is a live demonstration rather than a warning.** `customers NATURAL JOIN orders` returns 0 rows, because the shared column name is `id` and no order's id equals its customer's id, and `orders NATURAL JOIN countries` degrades to a 96-row cross product because the two share no column at all. Lesson 0007's author also checked that the "Don't Do This" wiki page does **not** mention `NATURAL JOIN`, contrary to what a Going-further bullet claimed, and replaced it with the note inside the primary source, which is where the warning actually lives.

### Stage 2 tooling and format findings

**A rendering defect class found here and now caught mechanically.** An answer that opens with the number it is answering, as in `64. Eight countries times eight customers`, is parsed as an ordered list starting at 64 and renders as a list item rather than the sentence it is. Two instances in lesson 0007 and one in lesson 0013 were fixed by writing the number as a word. The checker now flags a collapsible block whose first line opens with a number other than 1 and a full stop, since a deliberate list in an answer starts at 1; that rule found one pre-existing instance in the `finetuning` workspace, fixed with it, and no false positive across the five finished workspaces.

**On the overlap with stage 1.** Lesson 0004 already introduced the four set operations, their `ALL` variants and `EXCEPT` against `NOT EXISTS`, so lesson 0013 deliberately does not re-teach them: it supplies the verified counts on the real dataset, positional column matching, the two diagnostics, the `ORDER BY` placement rules, and the three-way comparison of the anti-join forms. A later reviser should not collapse the two lessons on the grounds that they share a subject.

### Stage 3

All run on PostgreSQL 18.6 with SQLite checks on 3.51. The baseline was rechecked first and had not moved: supported majors 14 to 18, current minor 18.6.

- **The default window frame is the stage's central fact, and it bites twice.** With an `ORDER BY` and no explicit frame the frame is `RANGE UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW`, and `RANGE` counts every row that ties on the ordering column, so `sum(amount) over (order by amount)` gives **20.00 to both** of the fixture's `10.00` orders while the `ROWS` spelling splits them 10.00 and 20.00 in an order nothing fixes. The same default is why `last_value` returns the current row's own value rather than the partition's last. Two lessons were built around those two consequences on purpose.
- **A frame restriction the spec did not know about**, found by lesson 0015's author running it: a `RANGE` bound with a numeric offset needs exactly one `ORDER BY` column, not merely an `ORDER BY`, and the failure is `ERROR: RANGE with offset PRECEDING/FOLLOWING requires exactly one ORDER BY column`, SQLSTATE `42P20`. Verified centrally afterwards. Only `ROWS` tolerates a window with no ordering at all.
- **The client is not `psql`, and three of its renderings differ**, which is now recorded because a lesson nearly pasted one. The driver prints a `boolean` as Python's `True`, a `jsonb` with single quotes, and a computed `numeric` zero as `0E-16` where the server prints `0.0000000000000000`. The harness was fixed to render booleans and JSON the server's way and to set the session to UTC, and the spec tells authors to append `::text` wherever the exact printed form matters. A `double precision` result also cannot be rounded directly: `round(percent_rank() over (...), 3)` fails with `42883` until it is cast to `numeric`.
- **`JSON_TABLE` and `jsonb_array_elements` produce identical rows**, verified side by side, and `JSON_TABLE` arrives with its columns already typed where the unnested form needs `(i ->> 'qty')::int`. Lesson 0019's author confirmed from the release notes that `JSON_TABLE` landed in PostgreSQL 17, and that `CYCLE` in a recursive query landed in 14.
- **`jsonb` normalises and `json` does not**: `'{"a": 1, "a": 2}'::jsonb` keeps the last key and `'{"b":1,"a":2}'::jsonb` reorders, while the same literals as `json` keep the original text. Lesson 0019 also found that `payload @> '{"items": []}'` matches every event with an `items` array, not only the empty one, since an empty array is vacuously contained.
- **The `LATERAL` error message teaches the feature better than prose does**: writing the correlated subquery in `FROM` without it gives `invalid reference to FROM-clause entry for table "c"` with a `HINT` naming `LATERAL` as the fix, SQLSTATE `42P01`. Lesson 0017 leads with the failure for that reason.
- **A window function cannot be filtered in `WHERE`** (`42P20`) or in `HAVING`, verified in both places, and the reason is lesson 2's evaluation order rather than a rule: the window is computed after both have run. A window over grouped rows does work, `sum(count(*)) over ()` beside a `GROUP BY`, which is the clearest demonstration of that ordering in the stage.
- **My brief to the sheet writer was wrong, and it checked rather than copied.** I told it that a `RANGE` or `GROUPS` offset bound needs exactly one `ORDER BY` column. Only `RANGE` does: `GROUPS BETWEEN 1 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW` runs happily with two ordering columns, and what `GROUPS` actually requires is an `ORDER BY` at all, failing with `GROUPS mode requires an ORDER BY clause` when there is none. Both verified centrally afterwards, and lesson 0015 had it right all along. Third stage in a row where a central brief was the thing that was wrong; the countermeasure stays the same, which is that every fact handed to an author carries the query that produced it.
- **The overlap between lessons 0017 and 0020 is deliberate and now says so.** Both compare a lateral top-1 against the window rewrite, 0017 on the largest order and 0020 on the newest, and the sheet writer flagged the framing as near-duplicate. It stays in both, because a reader meeting `LATERAL` needs the window alternative in that lesson rather than three lessons later, and 0017 carries the stop-early mechanism that 0020 does not re-derive. Lesson 0020 now credits 0017 explicitly and names what its own pass adds, the third form and the `NULLS LAST` correctness trap. Do not delete either half on the grounds that they look alike.
- **A machine leak found while verifying, and now caught mechanically.** A `timestamptz` printed in the session's zone carries the author's offset, and the Java arc's GC-log lesson had four lines stamped `+0300`. The offsets are now UTC, the numbers they sat beside are untouched, and the checker's machine-specific list covers that offset and the zone abbreviation so the next one cannot ship.

### Stage 4

All run on PostgreSQL 18.6, rechecked before writing and unchanged; supported majors 14 to 18. This stage has more version-sensitive material than any before it, so a reviser should treat an unversioned claim here as a bug.

- **Two things changed in PostgreSQL 18 that a schema written from older habits gets wrong.** `uuidv7()` exists, generating timestamp-ordered identifiers, verified as three successive values sharing a prefix and increasing where `gen_random_uuid()` values do not; on 14 to 17 there is no such function. And **a generated column with neither `STORED` nor `VIRTUAL` is now virtual**, verified as `attgenerated = 'v'` against `'s'`, with the release notes saying plainly that virtual "is now the default for generated columns". On 12 to 17 `STORED` was the only kind and the keyword was required. So the same DDL means two different things across supported releases, and every lesson that writes a generated column names which it means.
- **A `CHECK` accepts an absent value**, which is the single most useful thing in the stage and is lesson 3's rule arriving where it does damage: a constraint is violated only when its condition evaluates to false, and `NULL >= 0` is unknown. Verified with both a comparison and an `IN` list. The fix is `NOT NULL` beside the `CHECK`, or a condition that covers the absent case.
- **`RESTRICT` and `NO ACTION` raise different SQLSTATEs**, which the spec had not claimed and lesson 0023's author found: `RESTRICT` gives `23001` with a message naming the RESTRICT setting, while plain `NO ACTION` gives `23503`. The outcome is the same refusal, so the difference only shows in a log or an exception handler, which is exactly where it matters.
- **The anomalies were reproduced rather than described.** One `UPDATE` that set a new country name for `GB` touched three rows and left the table holding two spellings, because a typo row never matched the predicate. `LIKE '%red%'` over a comma-separated tag column matched a row whose tag was `infrared`. Updating one employee's department head left one department with two heads. Every normal form in lesson 0021 is introduced by the wrong answer it prevents.
- **Denormalisation's cost is a two-line demonstration.** A trigger-maintained counter tracked two inserts and one delete correctly, and then a plain `UPDATE` set it to 99 and the database accepted it, because nothing connects the column to the rows it counts. A `CHECK` can bound it, not verify it. That is why lesson 0026 orders its cases by who keeps the copy correct, and why it insists on a drift query.
- **`0A000` for a subquery in a generation expression** was found by lesson 0026's author rather than the spec: a generated column cannot see another row, and the engine says so in those words.
- **The exclusion-constraint failure a reader meets first is a missing operator class**, not a syntax error: on a fresh database `exclude using gist (room with =, during with &&)` fails with `42704` and a `HINT`, and `create extension btree_gist` fixes it. The release-18 shortcut `PRIMARY KEY (room, during WITHOUT OVERLAPS)` behaves the same way and has two extra rules: it needs at least two columns, `42601`, and it still needs that extension with a text key column, verified in a fresh database.
- **The native `enum` case is now settled with evidence rather than advice.** Lesson 1 told the reader to prefer `text` with a `CHECK` or a lookup table; the reasons are that a value added by `ALTER TYPE` cannot be used in the transaction that added it, `55P04` with a `HINT` saying so, and that removing one is not implemented at all, `0A000`. Renaming works. Lesson 0025 makes the three-way comparison on those grounds.
- **The cross-lesson audit found no contradictions and nothing that failed to reproduce**, which is the first stage where that happened. It confirmed lessons 0021 and 0026 acknowledge each other explicitly rather than merely disagreeing, and that lesson 0027 demonstrates each gap by inserting the wrong row before fixing the schema rather than asserting it. Its one substantive observation is worth keeping: the stage's promise is delivered with two disclosed exceptions, a cross-table equality that only a scheduled audit query catches and any rule spanning several statements over time, which is stage 5's.
- **An operational hazard of parallel authorship, worth carrying forward.** Seven authors share one database, and two of them needed a fresh-database state: one had to drop `btree_gist` to reproduce the missing-operator-class error and reinstall it afterwards, and another found `citext` already installed by an earlier verification run and correctly refused to treat that as a PostgreSQL fact. Both extensions have since been dropped, so the harness database matches a fresh one again. The next stage's spec should say that an extension check is part of reproducing an error, and that authors work in a schema named after their lesson number, which worked cleanly here: all seven dropped theirs.

### Stage 5

All run on PostgreSQL 18.6, and every interleaving below was deterministic on the first attempt. The two-session runner the stage was verified with is described under "Stage 5's decisions".

- **The recorded instruction to use attempt counts was wrong for this subject, and the evidence overturned it.** Every anomaly reproduced first time, every time, because the interleaving that produces it is one a client dictates statement by statement. Only the deadlock detector's choice of victim is genuinely unpredictable, and the lessons say so rather than guessing. If a later stage inherits a "reproduce the race" instruction, ask first whether the interleaving can simply be ordered.
- **PostgreSQL has no dirty read to demonstrate, at any level**, and the nuance worth teaching is that `current_setting('transaction_isolation')` still reports `read uncommitted` when you asked for it: the setting echoes the request while the behaviour is Read Committed. Lesson 0030 opens on a retrieval prompt for exactly this reason, which was the second recorded instruction for the stage and the right one.
- **Repeatable Read converts a lost update into an error rather than preventing the pattern.** Verified: at Read Committed the second writer blocks, then succeeds, and the first writer's change is gone with no error anywhere; at Repeatable Read the same interleaving fails the second writer with `could not serialize access due to concurrent update`, `40001`. The trade is the whole point, and lesson 0030 states it in one line.
- **Write skew is the anomaly that answers lesson 24's closing limit.** Two transactions each withdraw from a different row after reading the total, and at Repeatable Read both commit and the total is wrong while every per-row `CHECK` still holds. Serializable fails the second commit with `could not serialize access due to read/write dependencies among transactions`, its `DETAIL` naming a pivot and its `HINT` suggesting a retry.
- **A retry that replays is not a retry, and this is the stage's sharpest verified result.** After the Serializable failure, replaying the same statements commits and leaves exactly the total the anomaly would have produced, because the retried transaction never asked its question again. Lesson 0034's author sharpened the spec's wording here: the replayed `UPDATE` does read the current value, since it is an arithmetic update, so the accurate claim is that it never re-evaluated the rule, not that it never re-read. That correction is the difference between a lesson that is roughly right and one that is exactly right.
- **Errors that look alike and are not.** `NOWAIT` and `lock_timeout` both give `55P03`, `statement_timeout` gives `57014`, a deadlock gives `40P01`, a serialization failure gives `40001`, and a duplicate key gives `23505`, which is the one in that list a retry cannot fix: verified by retrying it and getting the identical error. `SHOW deadlock_timeout` is `1s`, so a deadlock costs at least that before anyone hears about it.
- **A unique index makes a writer wait**, verified: a second session inserting the same key blocks until the first commits, then fails with `23505`. So a deadlock needs no explicit lock at all, and lesson 0033 builds its second cycle out of two unique-index waits.
- **After a failed `COMMIT` at Serializable the transaction is already over**, verified centrally: the next statement in that session runs without a `ROLLBACK`, unlike the `25P02` state an ordinary failed statement leaves. Lesson 0030 originally showed a redundant `ROLLBACK` step, found by lesson 0034's author reading both, and it is now removed.
- **Two harness facts worth keeping.** The driver reports its own message rather than the server's if you send `COMMIT` to a session already in the aborted state, so an aborted-transaction demonstration has to use a plain statement, which does produce the server's `25P02`. And per-run numbers are everywhere in this material: process ids in a deadlock's `DETAIL`, transaction counters in `xmin` and `xmax`, snapshot values. The spec forbade pasting any of them and every author complied, describing the shape instead.
- **The cross-lesson audit found three things worth the pass.** A snapshot at Repeatable Read or Serializable is taken at the transaction's **first statement, not at `BEGIN`**, so a commit landing between the two is visible: verified centrally afterwards, and lesson 0030's phrasing implied the opposite and is now corrected. Lesson 0033 credited lesson 0032 with establishing that a unique index makes a second inserter wait, which 0032 never says, so the attribution is now to its own run. And lessons 0030 and 0031 both ran the full write-skew demonstration with identical numbers, which was my spec's fault for assigning it twice; lesson 0031 keeps the full interleaving and 0030 now states the Repeatable Read outcome in a sentence and points at it, keeping only the Serializable half that is its own subject.
- **One fact the sheet carries that no lesson states**: a lost update is refused at Serializable too, not only at Repeatable Read, with the same `40001`. The lessons are not wrong, since neither claims otherwise, but a reviser adding a level-by-anomaly claim should check the sheet first.
- **On lesson length.** Authors measure prose differently, and three reported figures 40 to 150 words above what the central count gives, which is why the canonical number is the central one. The closing lesson lands at about 1950 words by that count, above the 1800 cap, and it stays there: it carries the stage retrospective as well as its own material, which is what the Java and TypeScript closing lessons also did.

### Stage 6

All run on PostgreSQL 18.6 against the large fixture. Read "Stage 6's decisions" above first: it settles which numbers a lesson may quote, and this section is the evidence.

- **The stage's own premise held up.** Five runs of one query gave execution times spread by a few percent and an identical buffer count every time, and that is why every lesson quotes counts. Two authors independently re-measured the spread and got four and fourteen percent on different days, which is itself the argument: the spread is not a property of the query.
- **Release 18 changed `EXPLAIN`'s output in four ways**, confirmed in its release notes, and one of them turned out to matter more than expected. `Index Searches`, the new per-node count of index lookups, is **how a skip scan becomes visible**: on a table whose leading column has five distinct values, a query on the second column alone reports seven searches for one row, which is the scan restarting per leading value rather than reading the table. The optimisation and the line that reveals it arrived together.
- **An index does not stop being used when it stops being selective.** Widening a predicate to about half the table did not switch the plan to a sequential scan, as the folklore says; the planner kept the index and parallelised, giving a `Parallel Index Only Scan`. Lesson 0036 teaches that rather than the tidier story.
- **`CREATE STATISTICS` only fixes what it claims to.** Lesson 0037's author first tried `dependencies` statistics against a range condition and the estimate did not move; the documentation says those correct equality conditions only. The lesson demonstrates the equality case and says what the feature does not cover, which is a better lesson than the one the spec asked for.
- **Statistics are a sample, so their values are not reproducible the way a buffer count is.** The spec quoted exact `pg_stats` figures from one `ANALYZE`; three authors' runs gave different `n_distinct` values, different `correlation`, and one even surfaced a spurious `most_common_vals` entry the central run had not. Nothing was wrong: this is what sampling means. A statistics figure in a lesson is therefore described as an order of magnitude or an underestimate, while a buffer count is quoted exactly.
- **The pagination comparison is the stage's cleanest evidence and needs no timing at all**: the first page by index reads 20 rows and 4 buffers, the same page at `offset 500000` reads 500,020 rows and 4,993 buffers, and the keyset rewrite is back to 20 rows and 7 buffers.
- **Bloat, end to end, on a purpose-built table**: 200,000 rows at 27 MB become 54 MB after one `UPDATE` of every row, with 200,000 dead versions and about 46 percent dead space; a plain `VACUUM` clears the dead versions and leaves the file at 54 MB with half of it free for reuse; `VACUUM FULL` returns it to 27 MB and takes an exclusive lock. That is lesson 29's row versions arriving as a number on disk.
- **`pg_stat_statements` cannot be demonstrated without a server restart**, and the lesson shows the error rather than inventing output: `ERROR: pg_stat_statements must be loaded via "shared_preload_libraries"`, SQLSTATE `55000`. `pgstattuple` and `pg_buffercache` need no preload.

**The stage's real methodological lesson, which the next performance stage in any workspace should inherit: one shared database is the wrong tool for seven authors measuring plans.** Two things went wrong and both were caught. One author's lesson demonstrated statistics going stale by inserting five thousand rows **into the published fixture**, which is a defect for a reader as well, since fourteen earlier lessons quote those row counts; the rows were removed but the ten thousand dead versions they left shifted every buffer count on the table by sixty-four. Three lessons measured during that window, and their numbers were all exactly sixty-four buffers high. The other problem was quieter: authors' scratch indexes appeared and vanished on the same tables while others were capturing plans, so a plan could be captured that the clean fixture cannot produce.

What fixed it: the offending lesson was rewritten to build its own table, `VACUUM FULL ANALYZE` restored the fixture to a pristine layout, the canonical numbers were re-verified centrally, and the three affected lessons were re-measured by their own authors, who knew which query produced which number. The sheet writer's audit found the same drift independently, which is the check working. **For a future performance stage, give each author its own database, and forbid writing to a shared fixture in the spec rather than in a correction sent later**, which is where this spec was weak: it forbade writing to the small fixture and forgot to say the same about the large one.

### Stage 7

All run on PostgreSQL 18.6, with SQLite 3.51 for the portability lesson and SQLAlchemy 2.0.52 for the ORM one. Read "Stage 7's decisions" above first: it settles the source gap and the ORM choice, and this section is what the stage found.

- **The lock queue is the whole subject and it takes three sessions to see.** A long-running read holds its transaction open, an `ALTER TABLE` blocks behind it, and then an **ordinary `SELECT` blocks behind the `ALTER TABLE`** rather than behind the original transaction. So a migration that would have taken a millisecond stops every subsequent read on the table, and the DDL was never the slow part. Verified, and it is why `lock_timeout` before a migration is the guard rather than an optimisation.
- **`pg_relation_filenode` is the hardware-independent way to see a rewrite**, and the results are sharper than the folklore: adding a nullable column and adding a `NOT NULL` column with a **constant** default both leave the filenode alone, a volatile default such as `clock_timestamp()` rewrites, and among type changes `int` to `bigint`, `text` to `varchar(60)` and `bigint` to `numeric` rewrite while `varchar(60)` to `varchar(80)` and `varchar` to `text` do not.
- **Two lock levels the spec had not established, found by lesson 0043's author with `pg_locks`.** `ADD CONSTRAINT ... CHECK ... NOT VALID` takes `AccessExclusiveLock` and blocks even a plain `SELECT`, while `ADD CONSTRAINT ... FOREIGN KEY ... NOT VALID` takes only `ShareRowExclusiveLock`, which lets readers through and blocks writers. `VALIDATE CONSTRAINT` takes `ShareUpdateExclusiveLock` in both cases and blocks neither. The sheet writer added three more of its own: `DROP COLUMN` is `AccessExclusiveLock` with no rewrite, a one-step `ADD ... UNIQUE` scans without rewriting, and `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` followed by `ADD CONSTRAINT ... USING INDEX` avoids the scan.
- **The migration-tool trap is a SQLSTATE**: `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` fails inside a transaction block with `25001`, and a framework wraps each migration in a transaction by default, so the statement that exists to avoid blocking writes is the one that cannot run where migrations usually run. A failed concurrent build leaves an index reporting neither valid nor ready, which lesson 0042's author verified by building a unique index over a duplicate.
- **The ORM evidence is real emitted SQL, not a reconstruction.** Five customers and then their orders is **six statements**, `selectinload` is **two** with an `IN` list, `joinedload` is **one** with a `LEFT OUTER JOIN` off a subquery holding the `LIMIT`, and counting in the client emits a `SELECT` of every column where counting in the database emits one `count(*)`. All five reproduce from the probe script the stage was verified with.
- **Portability, re-run rather than inherited.** All six features the arc claimed travel to SQLite do, and all ten it claimed do not, fail as syntax errors. One extension the author found by running it: a `STRICT` table does reject text in an integer column, and it recognises only six base type names, so it refuses `NUMERIC(12,2)` outright at `CREATE TABLE` time.
- **One claim in this stage is cited rather than run, and says so**: that a validated `CHECK` lets `SET NOT NULL` skip its scan. Proving it needs a timing, which stage 6's rule forbids, so both lessons that mention it name release 12's notes as the source. One author tried to prove it from `pg_stat_user_tables` counters and correctly abandoned the attempt when the counters proved unreliable across sessions.

**Two gate-4 limitations worth recording, since both will recur.** A GitHub README anchor such as `#renaming-a-column` looks broken to an automated check, because GitHub emits the heading id as `user-content-renaming-a-column` and remaps it in the browser; verified, and the link is correct. And **two vendors block automated fetching outright**: `dev.mysql.com` and Medium both answer a scripted request with a 403. That is why the most-cited historical write-up on zero-downtime migration cannot be a source here, and why lesson 0046's MySQL citation was rescoped to MariaDB's documentation, which serves the same grammar fact and does answer; the lesson says so in the body rather than hiding it.

**The arc-level finding, from the sheet writer's last audit, and now fixed.** Stage 4 states that bad data becomes impossible, and lesson 0046 later shows that the promise is the engine's rather than the schema's, since SQLite's affinity typing lets a mistyped or unrounded value through the identical `CREATE TABLE`. A reader carried the stronger claim for nineteen lessons before that caveat arrived. Lesson 0027's "what the fixed schema still cannot promise" section now names it and points at 0046, which is where the caveat belonged all along.

**On lesson length across this arc.** Authors measure prose differently, and several reported figures 30 to 250 words above the central count, so the canonical number is the central one. Stage 7 lands between 1749 and 1957 words with two lessons a little over the 1800 cap, which matches what the Java and TypeScript arcs' later stages did: an applied review lesson and a closing lesson carry more than a mechanism lesson. Do not trim one to the cap at the cost of an example that was run.
